Fire Drill Documentation: What a Compliant Log Actually Looks Like

The drill happened. Everybody knows it happened. The alarm sounded, the residents moved to the assembly area, the staff did what they were supposed to do, and the building went back to normal in a few minutes.

Then a surveyor asks to see the last two years of fire drill records, and the whole thing comes down to what got written on a piece of paper. Not whether the drill was good. Whether it was documented in a way that proves it was good.

Fire drills are one of the most frequently cited documentation gaps in senior living, and it is almost never because the drills are not being run. It is because the record of the drill is thin, inconsistent, or missing the one field the surveyor most wants to see. This post walks through exactly what belongs on a compliant fire drill log, field by field, and why each one is there. There is a free template at the end you can print and start using today.

What the requirement actually is

For assisted living facilities in Alabama, fire drills must happen at least once a month, at varying times and on varying days, so the same shift is not always the one drilling. In group and congregate settings, drills also have to happen quarterly on each shift. Every drill must be initiated by the fire alarm system, and residents must actually move to their assembly areas.

For specialty care assisted living facilities, the bar is higher in one specific way that matters enormously for paperwork. Written observations of the effectiveness of each drill must be assessed monthly, filed, and kept for at least three years.

That phrase, written observations of effectiveness, is the heart of the whole thing. A surveyor is not satisfied by proof that a drill occurred. They want proof that someone watched it, judged how it went, and wrote that judgment down. A checkmark on a calendar does not do that. It is the single most common reason a facility that runs its drills faithfully still gets cited.

The fields a compliant log needs

Here is what every drill record should capture. Miss any of these and you have left a surveyor a question to ask.

Date and time. Both, not just the date. Part of the requirement is that drills happen at varying times, and the only way to prove that is a log that shows a spread of times across the year. A stack of records that all say 10:00 a.m. tells a surveyor the drills were scheduled for convenience, not realism.

Shift. Which shift ran the drill. This is what proves the quarterly-per-shift requirement is being met. If every drill on file was run by the day shift, the log itself is the evidence of the gap.

Whether the fire alarm system initiated it. The code requires drills to start from the alarm system, not from someone announcing "this is a drill." A yes-or-no field on this, marked yes every time, closes the question before it is asked.

Whether residents evacuated to the assembly area. A drill where residents stay put is not a drill. Record that they actually moved, and where they moved to.

Evacuation time. How long it took to get everyone to the assembly area. This is the number that turns a drill from a formality into a measurement. It also gives you a trend. If evacuation times are creeping up over the year, that is something you want to catch before a surveyor does.

Who participated. Staff on duty, and how many residents were present. Names or at least a count. This is what lets a surveyor connect the drill to the people who ran it.

Observations of effectiveness. This is the required field, and it is the one people skip. Write what you actually saw. "Evacuation smooth, all residents accounted for at assembly point within four minutes, one staff member unsure of the secondary route." That last clause is not a confession, it is exactly what makes the record credible. A log where every drill went perfectly for two years reads as a log nobody was really watching.

Problems identified and corrective action. If the observation noted something, this is where the follow-up goes. "Staff member re-trained on secondary evacuation route on the same day." A problem with a documented fix is a strength in a survey, not a weakness. A problem with no recorded follow-up is a finding.

Signature. The name and signature of the person who conducted the drill. An unsigned record is an anonymous one, and a surveyor cannot ask an anonymous record any questions.

The one field that gets facilities cited

If you take one thing from this, make it the observations field.

Every other field is a fact: a date, a time, a yes or a no. Those are easy to fill in and easy to fill in honestly. The observations field asks for judgment, and judgment takes a sentence or two of actual writing, which is exactly why it gets left blank or filled with a single word like "good."

But "good" is not an observation of effectiveness. It is the absence of one. A surveyor reading a year of drills that all say "good" learns nothing about whether anyone assessed those drills, which is the specific thing the code requires. Two honest sentences per drill, including the small things that did not go perfectly, is worth more than a year of clean checkmarks.

The goal is not a record that looks perfect. It is a record that looks like someone was paying attention.

Where to keep it

Wherever you keep these records, the test is the same one that applies to all survey documentation. When a surveyor asks for the last three years of fire drill logs, can you produce them in under five minutes, complete and legible?

A three-ring binder works if someone owns it and files every drill the day it happens. A shared folder works if every record actually makes it in. What does not work is the most common setup: some drills in a binder, some in a notebook in the maintenance shop, and the last few months in nobody's quite sure where. That is not a documentation system, it is a documentation hope.

The record only counts if it can be found. A perfect log that takes twenty minutes to locate is, for the length of that survey, a missing log.

The larger point

Fire drill documentation is not really about fire drills. It is a small, self-contained example of the pattern that runs through every survey: the work gets done, and the facility gets cited anyway, because the proof of the work was never captured in a form that holds up.

Fix it for fire drills and you have a template, literally and figuratively, for every other piece of documentation a surveyor will ask about. Capture it as it happens. Write down what you saw, not just that it occurred. Sign it. File it where it can be found. Do that, and the surveyor's request stops being a scramble and becomes a filing question with an easy answer.
